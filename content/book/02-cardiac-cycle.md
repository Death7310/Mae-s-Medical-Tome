## Defining the cycle

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of electrical and mechanical events from the start of one heartbeat to the start of the next. At a heart rate of 75 beats per minute each cycle lasts 0.8 seconds. Roughly 0.3 seconds of that is ventricular systole (contraction and ejection) and 0.5 seconds is ventricular diastole (relaxation and filling). When heart rate rises, both phases shorten, but diastole shortens far more than systole. At very high rates the time available for filling becomes the factor that limits stroke volume.

The cycle is triggered by the spontaneous depolarisation of the sinoatrial (SA) node. The impulse spreads through the atria, is delayed at the atrioventricular (AV) node for about 0.1 seconds, and then passes rapidly through the bundle of His, bundle branches and Purkinje fibres to the ventricles. The AV nodal delay is what allows the atria to finish contracting before the ventricles begin.

## Phases of the cycle

The description below follows the left heart, because the events on the right are identical in sequence and differ only in pressure.

### Atrial systole

Late in diastole the atria contract. By this point most ventricular filling has already happened passively, so atrial contraction adds only the final 20 to 30 per cent of the end-diastolic volume at rest. This contribution becomes more important at high heart rates and in ventricles that are stiff.

### Isovolumetric contraction

Ventricular depolarisation (the QRS complex) initiates ventricular contraction. As soon as ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure the mitral valve closes, producing the first heart sound (S1). For a brief period the ventricle is a closed chamber: the mitral valve is shut and the aortic valve has not yet opened. Pressure rises steeply while volume does not change, which is what the word isovolumetric means.

### Ejection

When left ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure (about 80 mmHg at rest) the aortic valve opens and ejection begins. During rapid ejection ventricular and aortic pressures rise together to a peak near 120 mmHg and most of the stroke volume leaves the ventricle. During reduced ejection the ventricle begins to repolarise (the T wave), the rate of outflow falls and pressure starts to decline.

### Isovolumetric relaxation

When ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure the aortic valve closes, producing the second heart sound (S2) and a small notch in the aortic pressure trace, the dicrotic notch (incisura). The ventricle is once more a closed chamber. Pressure falls rapidly while volume stays at the end-systolic value.

### Ventricular filling

When ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure the mitral valve opens and blood that has accumulated in the atrium during systole rushes in. This is the rapid filling phase, and it accounts for most of the end-diastolic volume. It is followed by diastasis, a period of slow filling in which flow is simply venous return passing through the atrium, and finally by atrial systole, which completes the cycle.

## Heart sounds

The heart sounds are caused by closure of the valves and the vibrations set up in the surrounding blood and tissue, not by the valves opening.

- **S1** ("lub") marks mitral and tricuspid closure at the start of ventricular systole. It is longer and lower pitched than S2.
- **S2** ("dub") marks aortic and pulmonary closure at the start of diastole. Normal inspiration delays pulmonary valve closure slightly, producing physiological splitting of S2.
- **S3** occurs in early diastole during rapid filling. It is normal in children and young adults but in older adults usually indicates a volume-overloaded ventricle, as in heart failure.
- **S4** occurs in late diastole during atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle. It is almost always pathological in adults.

## Reading the Wiggers diagram

The Wiggers diagram plots the electrocardiogram, aortic pressure, left ventricular pressure, left atrial pressure, left ventricular volume and the heart sounds against time on a shared axis. The skill to practise is reading the valve events off the pressure curves:

1. The mitral valve closes where the ventricular pressure curve crosses above the atrial pressure curve.
2. The aortic valve opens where the ventricular pressure curve crosses above the aortic pressure curve.
3. The aortic valve closes where the ventricular pressure curve crosses back below the aortic curve, marked by the dicrotic notch.
4. The mitral valve opens where the ventricular pressure curve crosses back below the atrial curve.

The atrial pressure trace shows three small waves: the *a* wave from atrial contraction, the *c* wave from the mitral valve bulging into the atrium during isovolumetric contraction, and the *v* wave from atrial filling against a closed mitral valve during ventricular systole. The same waves can be seen in the jugular venous pulse on the right side.

## Pressure–volume loops

If left ventricular pressure is plotted against left ventricular volume rather than against time, each cycle traces a loop that runs anticlockwise. The width of the loop is the stroke volume; the area inside it is the external stroke work. The four corners correspond to the four valve events. Increasing preload widens the loop to the right; increasing afterload makes it taller and narrower; increasing contractility shifts the upper left corner up and to the left along a steeper end-systolic pressure–volume relationship.
