## Why a pump and pipes

The cardiovascular system exists to move blood, and with it oxygen, nutrients, hormones, heat and waste, between the lungs, the gut, the kidneys and every tissue in the body. It is easiest to understand as two pumps arranged in series, the right heart and the left heart, feeding two circulations arranged in series, the pulmonary circulation and the systemic circulation. Blood that leaves the right ventricle must pass through the lungs before it can reach the left atrium; blood that leaves the left ventricle must pass through a systemic organ before it can return to the right atrium.

Because the two circulations are in series, the output of the right heart and the output of the left heart must be equal when averaged over more than a few beats. A persistent mismatch of even a few per cent would shift litres of blood into one circulation within minutes.

## Chambers, valves and the direction of flow

The heart has four chambers. The two atria are thin-walled receiving chambers. The two ventricles are the real pumps, and the left ventricle, which must drive blood through the long, high-resistance systemic circuit, has a wall roughly three times thicker than that of the right ventricle.

Four valves keep flow one-directional:

- The **atrioventricular (AV) valves** sit between atria and ventricles: the tricuspid valve on the right and the mitral (bicuspid) valve on the left. They open during ventricular filling and close at the start of ventricular contraction.
- The **semilunar valves** guard the ventricular outflow tracts: the pulmonary valve on the right and the aortic valve on the left. They open during ejection and close at the start of ventricular relaxation.

Valves open and close passively, in response to pressure differences across them. There is no nervous control of valve movement. The papillary muscles and chordae tendineae do not open or close the AV valves; they prevent the leaflets from everting into the atria during ventricular contraction.

## Cardiac output

Cardiac output (CO) is the volume of blood ejected by one ventricle per minute. It is the product of heart rate (HR) and stroke volume (SV):

> CO = HR × SV

At rest in a healthy adult, a heart rate near 70 beats per minute and a stroke volume near 70 mL give a cardiac output of roughly 5 L/min. During strenuous exercise in a trained person, cardiac output can rise to 25 L/min or more, mostly through an increase in heart rate with a smaller increase in stroke volume.

Stroke volume is the difference between the volume in the ventricle at the end of filling, the end-diastolic volume (EDV, about 120 mL at rest), and the volume left behind after ejection, the end-systolic volume (ESV, about 50 mL):

> SV = EDV − ESV

The **ejection fraction** is the proportion of the end-diastolic volume that is ejected, SV ÷ EDV. In a healthy resting adult it is roughly 55 to 70 per cent. A reduced ejection fraction is one of the defining measurements in heart failure.

## The three determinants of stroke volume

Stroke volume is governed by three variables that are worth learning as a group, because almost every drug and disease that changes stroke volume works through one of them:

1. **Preload** is the degree of stretch of the ventricular muscle at the end of diastole. In practice it is approximated by end-diastolic volume or pressure. Increased venous return raises preload.
2. **Afterload** is the load the ventricle must overcome to eject blood. For the left ventricle this is approximated by aortic pressure, or more precisely by ventricular wall stress during ejection.
3. **Contractility** (inotropy) is the intrinsic strength of contraction at a given preload and afterload. Sympathetic stimulation and circulating catecholamines raise contractility; many disease states and some drugs lower it.

The Frank–Starling mechanism describes the relationship between preload and stroke volume: within physiological limits, the more the ventricle is filled during diastole, the greater the volume it ejects during systole. This is the property that keeps the outputs of the right and left hearts matched beat to beat without any nervous involvement.

## Pressures around the circuit

Pressure falls continuously as blood moves away from the left ventricle. Typical resting values in a healthy adult are:

| Location | Typical pressure (mmHg) |
| --- | --- |
| Left ventricle | 120 / near 0 |
| Aorta and large arteries | 120 / 80 (mean about 93) |
| Arterioles | fall from about 85 to about 35 |
| Capillaries | about 35 falling to about 15 |
| Venae cavae and right atrium | 0 to 5 |
| Right ventricle | 25 / near 0 |
| Pulmonary artery | 25 / 8 (mean about 15) |

The steepest fall is across the arterioles. They are the main site of resistance to flow in the systemic circulation and, because their radius is under active control by smooth muscle, they are the main site at which the body regulates both blood pressure and the distribution of flow between organs.
