## Slide 3
sections: cv-overview#cardiac-output

The slide gives the equation; the book gives the sizes. Note that the resting figures (70 bpm, 70 mL, 5 L/min) are round numbers chosen to make the arithmetic obvious. The point that matters for the exam is that during exercise cardiac output rises mainly through **heart rate**, with a smaller rise in stroke volume, and that a reduced ejection fraction is a defining feature of one kind of heart failure.

## Slide 4
sections: cv-overview#the-three-determinants-of-stroke-volume

The table is a memory aid for the three levers on stroke volume. The book adds the detail the table drops: afterload is really ventricular **wall stress** during ejection, not aortic pressure, and the Frank–Starling mechanism is what keeps right and left output matched without any nervous control. Expect questions that give you a change (haemorrhage, a beta-blocker, aortic stenosis) and ask which lever it moves.

## Slide 5
sections: cardiac-cycle#phases-of-the-cycle, cardiac-cycle#defining-the-cycle, cardiac-cycle#reading-the-wiggers-diagram

The six-item list is the order of events. The book explains what defines the boundary between each phase: every boundary is a **valve opening or closing**, and every valve event is a pressure crossover on the Wiggers diagram. Learn the four crossovers in the "Reading the Wiggers diagram" section and the list on this slide becomes something you can reconstruct rather than memorise. The timing line (0.3 s versus 0.5 s) matters because diastole is what shrinks at high heart rates.

## Slide 6
sections: cardiac-cycle#heart-sounds

The slide's last line is the key idea: sounds come from closure. The book adds why S2 splits on inspiration and the clinical meaning of S3 and S4, which is the usual direction of exam questions. S3 in a 70-year-old points to volume overload; S4 points to a stiff, non-compliant ventricle.

## Slide 7
sections: blood-pressure#systolic-diastolic-pulse-and-mean-pressure, blood-pressure#the-haemodynamic-equation

Two formulas, two ideas. The MAP estimate uses one third of the pulse pressure because diastole occupies about two thirds of the cycle at rest; it becomes less accurate at high heart rates. The second formula, MAP ≈ CO × TPR, is the frame for every blood pressure question: identify whether the change acts on output or on resistance. The r⁴ relationship explains why small changes in arteriolar tone dominate resistance.

## Slide 8
sections: blood-pressure#short-term-regulation-the-baroreceptor-reflex

The slide compresses the reflex arc into one line. The book spells out the sensors, the afferent nerves (IX from the carotid sinus, X from the aortic arch), the integrating nucleus and the efferent response. The examinable subtlety is the last bullet: the receptors **reset** to the prevailing pressure within a day or two, so the reflex buffers acute swings but cannot explain or correct chronic hypertension.

## Slide 9
sections: blood-pressure#intermediate-and-long-term-regulation, blood-pressure#definitions-of-hypertension

The cascade on the slide is the standard sequence; the book adds the three triggers of renin release and the four actions of angiotensin II. The second bullet is Guyton's argument that the kidney's pressure–natriuresis curve sets long-term pressure. The guideline thresholds differ between the US and Europe; the book's table shows both so you can answer in whichever system a question uses.
