## Slide 3
sections: cv-overview#cardiac-output

The slide gives the equation; the book gives the sizes. Note that the resting figures (70 bpm, 70 mL, 5 L/min) are round numbers chosen to make the arithmetic obvious. The point that matters for the exam is that during exercise cardiac output rises mainly through **heart rate**, with a smaller rise in stroke volume, and that a reduced ejection fraction is a defining feature of one kind of heart failure.

### Question
Define cardiac output, state its equation with typical resting values, and explain what the ejection fraction tells you that stroke volume alone does not.

### Key points
- What cardiac output is: volume|amount|blood + per minute|each minute|a minute|every minute >> It is a rate. How much, per what unit of time?
- The equation: heart rate|hr + stroke volume|sv >> Two quantities multiply to give it.
- Resting size: 5 l|5l|five litres|five liters|5 litres|5 liters|5 liter|5 litre >> Roughly how many litres per minute at rest?
- Ejection fraction defined: ejection fraction|ef + end-diastolic|edv|end diastolic + fraction|proportion|percent|%|divided|ratio|per cent >> A proportion of which volume?
- Why ejection fraction matters: heart failure|failure|pump function|how well|reduced ejection|low ejection >> Which clinical diagnosis uses it?

### Model answer
Cardiac output is the volume of blood one ventricle ejects per minute. It equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume: at rest about 70 beats per minute and 70 mL per beat, which gives roughly 5 L/min. Stroke volume is the end-diastolic volume minus the end-systolic volume. The ejection fraction is stroke volume divided by end-diastolic volume, normally 55 to 70 per cent. It tells you how well the ventricle empties relative to how full it was, so a normal stroke volume from a dilated ventricle can still mean a low ejection fraction, which is one of the defining measurements in heart failure.

## Slide 4
sections: cv-overview#the-three-determinants-of-stroke-volume

The table is a memory aid for the three levers on stroke volume. The book adds the detail the table drops: afterload is really ventricular **wall stress** during ejection, not aortic pressure, and the Frank–Starling mechanism is what keeps right and left output matched without any nervous control. Expect questions that give you a change (haemorrhage, a beta-blocker, aortic stenosis) and ask which lever it moves.

### Question
Name the three determinants of stroke volume, define each in one sentence, then state the Frank–Starling mechanism and explain why it matters.

### Key points
- Preload defined: preload + stretch|end-diastolic|edv|filling|venous return >> It describes the state of the muscle at the end of diastole.
- Afterload defined: afterload + aortic pressure|wall stress|resistance|eject against|pressure the ventricle|must overcome >> What must the ventricle push against?
- Contractility defined: contractility|inotrop + intrinsic|strength|force|sympathetic|catecholamine|independent of preload|at a given preload >> Force at a given preload and afterload.
- Frank–Starling stated: frank|starling + fill|stretch|preload|edv|end-diastolic + eject|stroke volume|output|force of contraction|contracts more >> More in, more out, within limits.
- Why it matters: match|equal|balance|right and left|two ventricles|both ventricles|without nerv|beat to beat|no nervous >> What does it keep equal between the two sides, with no nervous control?

### Model answer
Preload is the stretch of the ventricular muscle at the end of diastole, approximated by end-diastolic volume, and it rises with venous return. Afterload is the load the ventricle must overcome to eject, approximated by aortic pressure or, more exactly, ventricular wall stress during ejection. Contractility is the intrinsic strength of contraction at a given preload and afterload, increased by sympathetic stimulation. The Frank–Starling mechanism states that, within physiological limits, the more the ventricle fills in diastole the more it ejects in systole. This matters because it keeps the outputs of the right and left ventricles matched beat to beat without any nervous involvement.

## Slide 5
sections: cardiac-cycle#phases-of-the-cycle, cardiac-cycle#defining-the-cycle, cardiac-cycle#reading-the-wiggers-diagram

The six-item list is the order of events. The book explains what defines the boundary between each phase: every boundary is a **valve opening or closing**, and every valve event is a pressure crossover on the Wiggers diagram. Learn the four crossovers in the "Reading the Wiggers diagram" section and the list on this slide becomes something you can reconstruct rather than memorise. The timing line (0.3 s versus 0.5 s) matters because diastole is what shrinks at high heart rates.

### Question
List the phases of the cardiac cycle in order. For each boundary between phases, name the valve event and the pressure crossover that causes it.

### Key points
- The order: atrial systole|atrial contraction + isovolumetric contraction + eject + isovolumetric relaxation + fill >> Six names in order; two of them share the word "isovolumetric".
- Mitral closes (S1): mitral|av valve|atrioventricular + clos + exceeds atrial|above atrial|greater than atrial|higher than atrial|rises above >> Which pressure rises above which to shut the inlet valve?
- Aortic opens: aortic|semilunar + open + exceed|above|greater than|higher than|80 >> What pressure must the ventricle beat to open the outlet?
- Aortic closes (S2): aortic|semilunar + clos + falls below|below aortic|drops below|less than aortic|dicrotic|incisura >> What mark does this leave on the aortic pressure trace?
- Mitral opens: mitral|av valve|atrioventricular + open + below atrial|falls below|less than atrial|lower than atrial >> Filling can begin only when which pressure is lower than which?
- Timing: 0.3|0.5|diastole shortens|shorter diastole|less time to fill|filling time|diastole is shortened >> Which phase shrinks most as heart rate rises, and what does that limit?

### Model answer
Atrial systole, isovolumetric contraction, ejection, isovolumetric relaxation, rapid filling and diastasis. The mitral valve closes, producing S1, when ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure; the aortic valve opens when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, about 80 mmHg; the aortic valve closes, producing S2 and the dicrotic notch, when ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure; and the mitral valve opens when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure. At 75 beats per minute systole lasts about 0.3 s and diastole about 0.5 s, and because diastole shortens most when heart rate rises, filling time becomes the limit on stroke volume at high rates.

## Slide 6
sections: cardiac-cycle#heart-sounds

The slide's last line is the key idea: sounds come from closure. The book adds why S2 splits on inspiration and the clinical meaning of S3 and S4, which is the usual direction of exam questions. S3 in a 70-year-old points to volume overload; S4 points to a stiff, non-compliant ventricle.

### Question
Explain what produces the first and second heart sounds and when each occurs in the cardiac cycle. Then say what S3 and S4 indicate and in whom.

### Key points
- What causes S1: s1|first heart sound|first sound + clos + mitral|tricuspid|atrioventricular|av valve >> Valves make a sound when they do one thing, not the other.
- What causes S2: s2|second heart sound|second sound + clos + aortic|pulmonary|pulmonic|semilunar >> Same event, different pair of valves.
- When each occurs: systole + diastole >> Each sound marks the start of a phase. Which phase for which sound?
- S3 meaning: s3|third + rapid filling|early diastole|young|children|volume overload|heart failure|volume-overloaded >> Normal in whom, and what does it suggest in an older adult?
- S4 meaning: s4|fourth + atrial|stiff|non-compliant|noncompliant|late diastole >> Which chamber is pushing blood into what kind of ventricle?

### Model answer
S1 is produced by closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves and marks the start of ventricular systole, because the valves shut as soon as ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure. S2 is produced by closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves and marks the start of diastole; inspiration delays pulmonary closure and splits S2 physiologically. Sounds come from closure, not opening. S3 occurs in early diastole during rapid filling: it is normal in children and young adults but in older adults usually indicates a volume-overloaded ventricle, as in heart failure. S4 occurs in late diastole when the atrium contracts into a stiff, non-compliant ventricle and is almost always pathological in adults.

## Slide 7
sections: blood-pressure#systolic-diastolic-pulse-and-mean-pressure, blood-pressure#the-haemodynamic-equation

Two formulas, two ideas. The MAP estimate uses one third of the pulse pressure because diastole occupies about two thirds of the cycle at rest; it becomes less accurate at high heart rates. The second formula, MAP ≈ CO × TPR, is the frame for every blood pressure question: identify whether the change acts on output or on resistance. The r⁴ relationship explains why small changes in arteriolar tone dominate resistance.

### Question
Define mean arterial pressure, estimate it for a reading of 120/80 and explain why the estimate uses one third of the pulse pressure. Then state the equation that links mean arterial pressure to cardiac output and say which vessels dominate the resistance term and why.

### Key points
- MAP defined: mean arterial|map + average|time-averaged|time averaged|drives|perfus|over the cycle|over the whole cycle >> Mean over what, and what does it drive?
- The estimate for 120/80: 93 >> Diastolic plus a third of the pulse pressure. Do the arithmetic.
- Why one third: diastole + longer|two thirds|2/3|more time|most of the cycle|twice as long >> Which phase occupies most of the cycle at rest?
- Pulse pressure: pulse pressure + systolic|sbp + minus|difference|−|less diastolic|take away >> It is the difference between which two numbers?
- The haemodynamic equation: cardiac output|co + resistance|tpr|svr >> Ohm's law for the circulation: pressure equals flow times what?
- Arterioles and radius: arteriole + radius|fourth power|r4|r⁴|r^4 >> Which vessels, and resistance varies with which power of the radius?

### Model answer
Mean arterial pressure is the time-averaged pressure over the cardiac cycle and is the pressure that drives organ perfusion. For 120/80 it is about 93 mmHg: diastolic pressure plus one third of the pulse pressure, and pulse pressure is systolic minus diastolic, 40 mmHg here. One third is used because diastole lasts roughly twice as long as systole at rest, so the mean lies closer to the diastolic value. Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output multiplied by total peripheral resistance. Arterioles dominate that resistance because resistance varies with the inverse fourth power of the radius, so small changes in arteriolar smooth-muscle tone produce large changes in resistance.

## Slide 8
sections: blood-pressure#short-term-regulation-the-baroreceptor-reflex

The slide compresses the reflex arc into one line. The book spells out the sensors, the afferent nerves (IX from the carotid sinus, X from the aortic arch), the integrating nucleus and the efferent response. The examinable subtlety is the last bullet: the receptors **reset** to the prevailing pressure within a day or two, so the reflex buffers acute swings but cannot explain or correct chronic hypertension.

### Question
Describe the arterial baroreceptor reflex as a reflex arc, naming the sensors, the afferent nerves, the integrating centre, the efferent outputs and the effect on blood pressure. Then explain why this reflex cannot set blood pressure in the long term.

### Key points
- Sensors: carotid sinus + aortic arch >> Two sites: one in the neck, one on a great vessel.
- Afferent nerves: glossopharyngeal|ix|cn ix|cn9|ninth + vagus|cn x|cn10|tenth >> Two cranial nerves, one per sensor.
- Integrating centre: nucleus tractus solitarius|nts|medulla|brainstem|brain stem >> Where do both nerves end?
- Efferent response: sympathetic + parasympathetic|vagal|vagus + heart rate|hr|contractility|dilat|constrict|resistance >> Two autonomic outputs move in opposite directions. What do they do to heart and vessels?
- Speed: second|beats|one or two|1-2|1–2|rapid|fast|immediate|within a beat >> How quickly does it act?
- Resetting: reset|adapt|accommodat + day|days|long-term|long term|chronic|hypertension|sustained >> What do the receptors do when pressure stays high for a day or two, and what does that imply for hypertension?

### Model answer
Stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and the aortic arch fire faster as arterial pressure rises. Carotid sinus signals travel in the glossopharyngeal nerve and aortic arch signals in the vagus nerve to the nucleus tractus solitarius in the medulla. When pressure rises, the medulla reduces sympathetic outflow and increases parasympathetic outflow, so heart rate, contractility and arteriolar and venous tone fall and pressure returns towards normal; a fall in pressure produces the opposite pattern. The reflex acts within one or two heartbeats, but the receptors reset within one to two days to whatever pressure they are exposed to, so they buffer minute-to-minute swings and cannot set the long-term level. Chronic hypertension is therefore not a baroreceptor failure.

## Slide 9
sections: blood-pressure#intermediate-and-long-term-regulation, blood-pressure#definitions-of-hypertension

The cascade on the slide is the standard sequence; the book adds the three triggers of renin release and the four actions of angiotensin II. The second bullet is Guyton's argument that the kidney's pressure–natriuresis curve sets long-term pressure. The guideline thresholds differ between the US and Europe; the book's table shows both so you can answer in whichever system a question uses.

### Question
Trace the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone cascade from the stimuli for renin release to the actions of angiotensin II. Then explain in one or two sentences why the kidney, rather than the baroreflex, sets long-term blood pressure.

### Key points
- Renin triggers: juxtaglomerular|jg cell|afferent arteriole + perfusion|low pressure|reduced pressure|macula densa|sodium|nacl|salt|sympathetic|beta >> Which cells release it, and what signals do they respond to?
- The cascade: angiotensinogen + angiotensin i|ang i|angiotensin 1|angiotensin-i + ace|converting enzyme + angiotensin ii|ang ii|angiotensin 2|angiotensin-ii >> Four molecules, two enzymes, in order.
- Angiotensin II actions: vasoconstrict|constrict + aldosterone + adh|antidiuretic|vasopressin|thirst|sodium reabsorption|na reabsorption|reabsorb >> Actions on vessels, on the adrenal cortex, and on salt and water.
- Pressure natriuresis: natriuresis|pressure-natriuresis|pressure natriuresis + excret|sodium|salt|water|urine + volume|blood volume|output|falls|fall >> Raise the pressure: what does the kidney do, and what happens to volume and output?
- Guideline thresholds: 130|140 + 80|90 >> Two guideline systems, two thresholds.

### Model answer
Juxtaglomerular cells in the afferent arteriole release renin when renal perfusion pressure falls, when less sodium chloride reaches the macula densa, or under β1 sympathetic stimulation. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which angiotensin-converting enzyme on pulmonary endothelium converts to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II constricts arterioles, stimulates aldosterone and antidiuretic hormone release, provokes thirst and increases renal sodium reabsorption, so it raises both resistance and volume over minutes to hours. In the long term the kidney dominates through pressure natriuresis: a sustained rise in pressure increases sodium and water excretion, blood volume and cardiac output fall, and pressure returns to the level at which excretion matches intake. Hypertension is defined from 130/80 mmHg in the ACC/AHA system and 140/90 mmHg in the ESC/ESH system.
