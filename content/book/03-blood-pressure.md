## Systolic, diastolic, pulse and mean pressure

Arterial pressure is pulsatile. The peak during ejection is the **systolic pressure** (SBP) and the trough just before the next ejection is the **diastolic pressure** (DBP). A healthy resting value is close to 120/80 mmHg.

Two derived numbers matter more than either reading alone:

- **Pulse pressure** = SBP − DBP, normally about 40 mmHg. It rises when stroke volume increases or when the large arteries become stiffer, which is why pulse pressure widens with age.
- **Mean arterial pressure (MAP)** is the time-averaged pressure over the cycle and is the pressure that actually drives organ perfusion. Because diastole lasts longer than systole at rest, MAP lies closer to diastolic than to systolic pressure and is usually estimated as:

> MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓ (SBP − DBP)

For 120/80 this gives about 93 mmHg. A MAP of roughly 60 to 65 mmHg is usually regarded as the minimum needed to perfuse the brain and kidneys, which is why that threshold appears in resuscitation targets.

## The haemodynamic equation

Flow through any vascular bed is pressure difference divided by resistance, the circulatory equivalent of Ohm's law. Applied to the whole systemic circulation:

> MAP − CVP = CO × TPR

where CVP is central venous pressure (near zero) and TPR is total peripheral resistance. In practice, MAP ≈ CO × TPR. Every factor that changes mean arterial pressure does so through cardiac output, through peripheral resistance, or through both. Since cardiac output is heart rate × stroke volume, the full set of levers is heart rate, preload, contractility, afterload and arteriolar tone.

Resistance in a vessel depends on its length, the viscosity of the blood and, above all, its radius. Poiseuille's relationship shows resistance varies with the inverse fourth power of the radius, so halving the radius of an arteriole increases its resistance sixteen-fold. This is why arteriolar smooth muscle, which can change radius quickly, is the body's main short-term control of resistance.

## Short-term regulation: the baroreceptor reflex

The arterial baroreflex is the fastest regulator of blood pressure, acting within one or two heartbeats.

**Sensors.** Stretch receptors in the wall of the carotid sinus (just above the bifurcation of the common carotid artery) and in the aortic arch fire at a rate that increases with arterial pressure. They are most sensitive in the range 60 to 180 mmHg and respond more strongly to a rising pressure than to a steady one.

**Afferents.** Carotid sinus signals travel in the glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve IX, via the carotid sinus nerve); aortic arch signals travel in the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X). Both end in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) of the medulla.

**Integration and efferents.** When pressure rises, increased baroreceptor firing causes the NTS to inhibit sympathetic outflow and to increase parasympathetic (vagal) outflow to the heart. The result is slowing of the heart, reduced contractility, arteriolar dilatation and venodilatation, all of which lower pressure. A fall in pressure produces the opposite pattern.

**Limitation.** The baroreceptors reset within one to two days to whatever pressure they are exposed to. They therefore buffer minute-to-minute swings (standing up, straining, emotion) but cannot set the long-term level of arterial pressure. Chronic hypertension is not a baroreceptor failure.

Other rapid mechanisms include the cardiopulmonary (low-pressure) receptors in the atria and great veins, the chemoreceptor reflex, which becomes important when pressure falls below about 80 mmHg, and the CNS ischaemic response, a last-resort massive sympathetic discharge at pressures below about 60 mmHg.

## Intermediate and long-term regulation

**Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS).** Juxtaglomerular cells in the afferent arteriole of the kidney release renin in response to reduced renal perfusion pressure, reduced sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa, or β1-adrenergic sympathetic stimulation. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen (from the liver) to angiotensin I, which angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE, abundant on pulmonary endothelium) converts to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II is a potent arteriolar constrictor and stimulates aldosterone release from the adrenal cortex, antidiuretic hormone release, thirst and direct renal sodium reabsorption. The system acts over minutes to hours and sustains pressure during volume loss.

**Renal pressure natriuresis.** Over days to weeks, the kidney dominates. A rise in arterial pressure increases renal excretion of sodium and water; the resulting fall in blood volume reduces cardiac output and pressure until excretion once again matches intake. In Guyton's analysis this makes the renal pressure–natriuresis relationship the ultimate determinant of the long-term level of arterial pressure: anything that sustains hypertension must shift this relationship to a higher pressure.

## Definitions of hypertension

Guidelines differ, and it is worth knowing which one an examiner or clinic uses.

| Guideline | Normal | Elevated / high-normal | Hypertension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ACC/AHA 2017 (USA) | < 120/80 | 120–129 / < 80 | Stage 1: 130–139 or 80–89; Stage 2: ≥ 140 or ≥ 90 |
| ESC/ESH 2018 (Europe) | 120–129 / 80–84 | 130–139 / 85–89 | Grade 1: 140–159 / 90–99; Grade 2: 160–179 / 100–109; Grade 3: ≥ 180 / ≥ 110 |

Both systems require readings on more than one occasion, and both favour out-of-office (home or ambulatory) measurement to confirm the diagnosis and to detect white-coat and masked hypertension.
